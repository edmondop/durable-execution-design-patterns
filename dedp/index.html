Preface

On Patterns and Why Engineers Struggle with Them

Most software engineers first encounter design patterns in a university course. You build a toy project, memorize some UML diagrams, implement a Singleton to satisfy a homework assignment, and move on. The projects are too small for the patterns to matter — there is no team to coordinate with, no codebase that has outgrown one person’s comprehension, no production system where a bad abstraction means six months of rework. Patterns feel like ceremony in that context: verbose, overly formal, solutions looking for problems.

I know this because I lived it. When I first encountered pattern catalogs, I found them archaic. The class diagrams felt like relics of an era obsessed with inheritance hierarchies. The prose was dense. I could not connect any of it to the code I was actually writing. For years I dismissed the whole idea.

What changed my mind was scale. When the scope of a system exceeds what you can hold in your head — seven services, four teams, a timeline measured in quarters — you need a way to communicate structural ideas quickly. Not implementation details, but the shape of a solution, to people who will go build their piece of it independently. A three-page design document explaining how your system coordinates distributed actions with paired rollbacks that unwind in reverse order on failure takes a meeting, a whiteboard session, and three rounds of review comments to land. Saying "this is a Saga" and pointing to a well-documented pattern communicates the same thing in ten seconds.

In a presentation I gave on engineering communication, I argued that diagrams are one of the most underused tools in our profession. Civil, mechanical, electrical, and architectural engineers all use shared diagrams as their primary medium for planning and review. Research backs this up: people skim text in F-shaped patterns (Nielsen Norman Group), but they engage with diagrams holistically. Diagrams reduce ambiguity, force precision, and make hidden assumptions visible. Patterns serve the same function at a higher level of abstraction. A diagram communicates the structure of one system; a pattern communicates the structure of an entire class of systems. When a team shares a pattern vocabulary, they can reason about architecture the way structural engineers reason about trusses — by naming the form, not describing each beam.

How I Discovered Durable Execution

I spent a large part of my career in financial services, where workflows are fundamental. Transaction processing, compliance checks, loan origination, payment reconciliation — these are long-running stateful processes that must survive failures, handle human intervention, and maintain audit trails. Every company in the space builds some version of a workflow engine, whether they call it that or not.

When I became CTO and co-founder of a fintech startup in 2016, we built the first version of our platform on a Java workflow engine from the BPM world. Business logic lived in BPMN XML files — diagrams that compiled into execution graphs. The engine ran inside a servlet container, and you could not test a workflow without standing up the full stack: application server, database, the engine itself. There was no way to write a unit test that exercised just the process logic. The engine exposed a REST API, but there were no language SDKs outside Java — which meant every non-Java service had to talk to it through raw HTTP calls with no type safety, no IDE support, and no way to catch contract changes at compile time. In practice, it was a tool designed for the J2EE era: perfectly at home inside an enterprise monolith, deeply foreign to a polyglot microservice architecture. The engine worked well for what it was designed to do, but when the business needed behavior that BPMN could not express natively, you had to reach for escape hatches: custom Java delegates, REST calls that returned routing decisions, or the external task service pattern. All workable, but each one pulled you further from the visual process model that was supposed to be the point.

We eventually replaced it with a choreography-based architecture: services communicating through events, each owning its own slice of the business process. This solved the monolith problem but introduced a different set of failures. Observability disappeared overnight — no single place showed the state of a loan application as it moved through underwriting, compliance, and disbursement. Error handling became inconsistent across teams. One team would retry failed steps; another would dead-letter them; a third would silently drop them and rely on a daily reconciliation batch to catch the gaps. Compensating actions were implemented ad hoc, and the subtle bugs they introduced surfaced months later in production, usually during an audit.

When I discovered durable execution, it collapsed the gap between what I wanted to express and what the platform could support. Write sequential code, and the runtime handles persistence, retries, and failure recovery. The state lives in the code, not in a database schema you maintain separately. The workflow is the program. I had spent years building infrastructure to paper over the distance between business logic and execution guarantees. Durable execution simply removed that distance.

Who Is This Book For?

This book is for backend engineers and architects who build distributed systems — whether you’re already using a durable execution platform or evaluating one. You should be comfortable reading code in at least one of Go, Java, Python, TypeScript, or C#. Familiarity with concepts like retries, idempotency, and eventual consistency will help, but the chapters explain what you need as they go.

If you’ve never heard of durable execution, the Introduction provides the context. If you’re already building with Temporal or a similar platform, the pattern catalog gives you a vocabulary for the recurring designs you’ve likely encountered but haven’t named.

What This Book Is (and Is Not)

This book is about durable execution patterns, not about any specific platform.

A durable execution platform lets you build things that are genuinely difficult without one: long-running stateful processes, distributed coordination, failure recovery, exactly-once semantics. These capabilities enable recurring structural solutions — patterns — that are worth naming, documenting, and reusing.

The examples use Temporal. I am a member of the Temporal Constellation program, and I chose Temporal because it is the most flexible and complete implementation of the durable execution model available today. Each chapter uses a different Temporal SDK language — Go, Java, Python, TypeScript, .NET — to show that these patterns are not language-specific.

The patterns themselves are platform-agnostic. Other durable execution platforms — Restate, DBOS, Azure Durable Functions, Infinitic, and others — make different design decisions and support different subsets of these patterns. Some patterns require capabilities — such as external messaging into workflows, synchronous state inspection, and history compaction — that not every platform provides. The consequences section of each pattern discusses these constraints honestly.

For an up-to-date list of platforms, I maintain Awesome Durable Executions, an open-source catalog of platforms, tools, and resources in this space.

I hope this book gives you a vocabulary that makes your distributed systems clearer to design, easier to communicate about, and less painful to operate.
